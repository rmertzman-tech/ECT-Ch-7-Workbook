1 · Arrive

Two things that are both real

A friend asks you to cover a shift so they can go to a family event. Taking it means you're exhausted for your own commitment tomorrow. Not taking it means they miss something that matters. There's no error here — you're caught between what the outcome will actually be, and what this specific person, right in front of you, needs.

2 · Two Answers, Both Right

Consequentialism and Care, named precisely

Consequentialism asks what actually happens to real people as a result of a decision. Care ethics asks what a specific relationship, with a specific person's specific need, requires of you right now. The difficulty isn't that one is mistaken — they're answering different questions, and a single decision only lets you act on one answer at a time.

Applied to the shift-covering scenario: what does each tradition actually ask, and why aren't you simply confused when you feel both at once?

3 · The Authorship Check

Five questions, named for the first time

Terrain Check: Does my map of this situation actually match what is really there?
Coherence Check: Does this conclusion actually fit with the rest of what I believe?
Gut Check: Does this actually feel right underneath the reasoning — and if not, which one has to give?
Weight Check: How much does this decision actually matter, at the scale it actually operates at?
Capacity Check: Given my actual state right now, am I actually capable of doing this well?

These five aren't a checklist to run every time. Most decisions only need one or two. Which one do you find yourself skipping most often, and what does that tend to cost you?

4 · The Capacity Check, Deepened

Not "am I too tired" — where does my energy belong?

The Capacity Check, done fully, weighs your full ABCD state — Agency, Beauty and Awe, Context and Contingency, Dynamism — against real constraints of time and energy, "all things considered." A meta-cognitive prosthetic, named here for the first time, is a tool built to help a bounded mind do consciously what it can't do by unaided instinct: weigh all four against each other under real limits.

Given everything true about your ABCD state right now, what is actually the wisest use of your next hour of real capacity?

5 · Tim Palmer

The ethics of uncertainty

Tim Palmer helped build ensemble forecasting: running dozens of atmospheric models with slightly varied starting conditions and reporting the full distribution, rather than one confident-sounding line. "Rain: 40 percent" is a forecast you can actually use. A forecast that quietly rounds 40 percent down to "no rain" is not more confident — it's less honest, and it has a body count.

Why does presenting a single-track forecast as certain count as a moral choice, not just a neutral technical shortcut?

6 · Doyne Farmer and Burden Export

What the average person can't see

Standard economic models relied on a "representative agent" — one idealized person standing in for an entire economy. A lockdown's effect on a representative agent is smooth. Its real effect on a restaurant worker, a remote engineer, a nurse, and a family without savings is entirely different — and the people who fall furthest from the average are the ones the model can't see at all.

Burden Export: what happens when a system becomes more efficient specifically by transferring its own cost onto people it does not track — often a sign of success by the system's own metrics, not failure.

Name a real or plausible case where something looks efficient specifically because its real cost has been pushed onto people the accounting doesn't track.
